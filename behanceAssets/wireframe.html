Case Study — UX / Product Design

Why does formatting a thesis cost a week and a guess?

FormaTexto replaces a slow, opaque human formatting market with an AI pipeline students can actually trust — priced upfront, scoped to exactly what they need, and never a black box. This case study is about the reasoning, not the framework.

Problem framing Trust & pricing UX Solo / founder-led Brazil academic market

The problem

A black box, twice over

This started from direct experience, not a research study. Formatting a thesis to a specific academic standard — ABNT NBR 14724, for most Brazilian universities — is mechanical, rule-heavy work with nothing to do with the writing itself: margins, heading hierarchy, references, figure captions, checked against a style guide most students open for the first time under deadline pressure.

The existing alternative is a cottage industry of human formatting services. I knew this market directly: they quote per job, not per page, so a student has no idea what they'll pay until a stranger has already opened the file. Turnaround runs one to two weeks. There's no "just fix the references chapter" — it's the whole document or nothing.

^The problem wasn't that formatting is annoying. It's that the only fast, cheap fix is a black box you have to trust blind — an unknown price, an unknown wait, and a stranger rewriting a document you're about to defend.

Who this is for

What they actually need

No formal interviews — this is reasoned from having been this user, and from watching the existing market fail them. Naming that plainly rather than dressing it up as research that didn't happen.

^

"I need to know the cost before I commit, not after someone reviews my file."

Price anxiety is real when a student is paying out of pocket — an opaque quote is a reason not to start at all.

^

"I don't want the whole document touched if I only need help with one part."

All-or-nothing pricing charges for work nobody asked for.

^

"I need to trust an AI won't quietly rewrite my voice in a document I have to defend out loud."

This is an academic document under scrutiny, not a blog post — confident, silent rewrites are a liability.

^

"My school has one specific standard — 'formatted nicely' isn't the deliverable."

Compliance with a named norm (ABNT, APA, MLA, Chicago) is the actual requirement, not a generic tidy-up.

^

"I'm on a deadline — I can't afford back-and-forth, but I don't want to pay before I know this works."

Speed matters, but so does a way to de-risk the first try.

^

"If the tool gets stuck, I don't want to lose my place and start over."

A rigid pipeline that hard-fails on the first edge case is worse than a slow human who'd just ask a question.

^

"This is my unpublished thesis — I want to know it isn't sitting on a server indefinitely."

Privacy isn't an afterthought when the document is unpublished academic work.

Design principles

What fell out of that list

Four principles shaped every screen that followed.

1

The price on screen one is the price at checkout.

No quote, no review period, no gap between what a user sees and what they pay.

2

Granularity, not all-or-nothing.

The unit of work — and of price — is a "lauda" (~300 words), not "the document." Users choose exactly what gets processed.

3

Show the work, don't just hand back a file.

Every step that touches the document renders it — the actual pages, not an icon or a filename — so nothing is a black box.

4

Fail soft, not hard.

When the pipeline can't resolve something on its own, it flags it and keeps going, rather than blocking the whole document.

Needs → decisions

The line from need to screen

The part worth showing isn't the screens — it's the reasoning from a specific need to a specific, sometimes non-obvious, decision, including what got rejected along the way.

User needDecisionWhy this, not the obvious alternative
Know the cost upfront Per-lauda pricing, running total from screen one through checkout The market norm here is "submit for a quote." Rejected outright — a visible price is the whole point of not being a human service.
Don't pay for scope I didn't ask for Lauda-by-lauda selection, not whole-document-only Considered defaulting to "process everything" for simplicity. Rejected — it silently overcharges anyone who only needs part of a thesis fixed, which is common (an advisor already reviewed the methodology; only the references need work).
Trust the AI with my academic voice Copy states explicitly what it won't do; page-selection renders the real document, not a summary A generic "AI-powered editing" pitch reads as risk, not benefit, to this user. Naming the restraint directly does more for trust than a longer feature list.
Match a named standard, not vague "nice formatting" An explicit guideline picker (ABNT, APA, MLA, Chicago) surfaced as soon as formatting is selected Could have shipped one default style. Didn't — "close enough" doesn't pass a committee, so the standard itself needed to be a visible, first-class choice.
De-risk trying an unfamiliar service before paying One free lauda, no card required, stated on the primary CTA itself Free trials are usually hidden behind signup friction. Put it on the button — the biggest drop-off risk isn't price, it's "will this work on my document," and that has to be answerable before any payment screen.
Don't lose my progress if something's ambiguous A needs_input state: a placeholder for an uninferrable caption or source, flagging only that gap The two easy defaults — guess silently, or refuse the file — both fail the user. Guessing risks a wrong citation in a defended thesis; refusing wastes the time already spent.
Know my document won't sit on a server forever Retention notice restated at upload and at checkout, not left to the Terms of Service Legal coverage in a ToS clause doesn't address the anxiety in the moment someone uploads an unpublished thesis. Restating it at the two points of highest hesitation does.

Walking the flow

Every screen, tied back to a need

Account creation

Sign-up asks for the password twice and flags a mismatch inline, next to the field, before the form is ever submitted. Small on its own, but on a page whose entire job is lowering the friction to trust a tool with an academic document, a rejected-after-submit password error is an unnecessary stumble at the worst possible moment.

New service — services, guideline, upload

One scrolling page, not a wizard — splitting "pick a service," "pick a standard," and "upload" across separate pages would have hidden the total price behind extra clicks, working against principle 1. The submit button stays disabled until every required input is set: deliberate friction against an incomplete order, not an oversight.

Lauda selection

The screen doing the most work against the trust problem. Left rail: one-click include/exclude per lauda — principle 2 made concrete. Center: the actual document rendered, so the user is looking at their pages, not a generic preview — principle 3. Pré-textual pages (cover, title page, abstract, table of contents) now render as their own labeled cards ahead of the numbered laudas, so nobody wonders why "Lauda 1" isn't page 1. Right: the price stays live as selections change, so the number never lags behind the decision that produced it.

Checkout

Deliberately boring: a line-item summary matching the previous screen exactly, a restated retention notice, then payment. Any new information here would break principle 1. A returning user with a saved card pays in one click; "Usar novo cartão" stays visible as an explicit escape hatch, and saving a card for next time is opt-in, never assumed.

Dashboard — empty state

First thing a new user sees post-signup. States the empty condition directly and repeats the primary action, so there's no dead end.

Project detail — original vs. processed

Where "show the work" matters most (principle 3). Renders original and processed documents side by side rather than only offering a download, so the result can be verified before it's trusted. Any needs_input gap (principle 4) surfaces grouped by type — tables, then figures — each with a field to type the missing text directly, or an amber "deixar em branco" action that requires a separate confirmation. Skipping a caption is a choice the interface makes you confirm, not an accidental empty field.

Honest assessment

What's unvalidated

None of the above has been tested with real users yet. The needs list is reasoned from firsthand experience and close knowledge of the existing market, not from interviews or usability sessions — worth saying plainly, since pretending otherwise would undercut everything above it.

The honest next step: put the get-started → lauda-selection flow in front of five actual thesis-writing students and watch where principle 2 holds up against what people actually predict about their own document — do they already know which laudas need work before seeing them rendered, or does the screen itself teach them that? That's the test that would tell me whether the granularity idea is doing what I think it's doing.
